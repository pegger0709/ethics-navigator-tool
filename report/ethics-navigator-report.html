Ethics Navigator: A Local-First Retrieval System for Neuroethics Guidance

Design, architecture, and evaluation of the conversational successor to the rules-based Navigation Tool

Author

Philip Egger

Published

August 24, 2026

Executive Summary

NoteDraft status

Written last, as an abstract of the sections that follow. Figures for corpus composition and the model trade-off are generated from live project data; the evaluation numbers are from the measured runs described in Section 4.

The Ethics Navigator is a locally-hosted question-answering system that lets a neurotechnology company interrogate the ethical and legal instruments that govern its work — UNESCO’s neurotechnology recommendation, the OECD’s responsible-innovation principles, the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, and the jurisdiction-specific regimes of the EU and California.

It is the successor to a rules-based Navigation Tool piloted at the Asilomar workshop in March 2026 (IDUN Technologies and Ningen Neuroethics Co-Lab 2026). That earlier tool asked a fixed questionnaire and returned red flags. Workshop participants rejected it for rigidity: the questions felt “too academic, obvious, or leading”, the scope was “too narrowly focused on partnerships”, and it “doesn’t feel like it fits into a real workflow”. The most frequently requested replacement was, in participants’ own words, an “interactive ‘chatbot’ type portal” and an “AI-Agent as process facilitator”. This system is that pivot: from a fixed decision tree to a grounded conversation over primary sources.

The system runs entirely on the user’s own machine. Documents, questions, and answers never leave it — a requirement, not a preference, given that the questions a company asks an ethics tool are themselves commercially and legally sensitive.

Headline results. Against a seven-case gold dataset, the system answers 6 of 7 correctly. On the hardest case — an open request to summarise every principle governing responsible neurotechnology development — an independent judge model scores the answer 14 out of 14 on a rubric of principles the corpus actually states. The single reproducible failure is a retrieval defect, not a reasoning one, and it reproduces identically across four different language models, which localises it precisely.

Principal engineering finding. Nearly every quality problem encountered during development was a retrieval problem masquerading as a model problem. Fixing document chunking doubled retrieval recall at fixed cost; adding pre-computed per-document digests took the broad-synthesis case from unanswerable to full marks. Changing the language model, by contrast, moved the headline score comparatively little (Section 4.4).

Introduction

Problem Statement

A neurotechnology company faces a proliferation of ethical and regulatory guidance with, as the workshop framing put it, “unclear implementation pathways”. The instruments are public, lengthy, and written for regulators rather than founders. UNESCO’s recommendation alone runs to over a hundred thousand characters across nine principles, seven values, and twelve areas of policy action; the EU AI Act is five times longer again.

The practical question a founder asks is not “what does Article 9 say” but “what applies to us, and what must we actually do about it”. Answering that today requires either in-house neuroethics expertise — which the workshop identified as absent in most companies — or expensive external counsel.

The system addresses a narrow, tractable slice of this: making the primary sources directly interrogable in natural language, with answers grounded in and attributed to the documents themselves.

Motivation

The motivation is documented rather than assumed. The predecessor tool was tested by practitioners at the Asilomar workshop, and its shortcomings were recorded in structured feedback (IDUN Technologies and Ningen Neuroethics Co-Lab 2026).

Five criticisms were reported back to the room, and they map cleanly onto design requirements for a successor:

Workshop finding Design consequence
Questions feel too academic, obvious, or leading Let the user pose the question; do not script it
UX friction, limited interaction options Conversational interface, not a form
EU/Western regulatory framing may not translate globally Explicit jurisdiction selection; global instruments always in scope
Scope too narrowly focused on partnerships Answer any question the corpus can support
Doesn’t fit a real workflow Run locally, answer on demand, no orchestration required

The free-text responses were more specific still. One table asked for “a more vector based overall tendence rather than a red flag for a not checked box”; another for a shift “from yes no to text / process”. A third argued the tool should “describe the potential risk exposure of the partnership” instead of emitting red flags at the end. Several converged independently on an AI facilitator that could “adapt to circumstances as ethical boundaries and technologies change”.

Two cautions from the same feedback are treated here as binding constraints rather than aspirations. First: “This tool doesn’t replace the need for more education, collaboration, and experts (human).” Second: the tool should not “create new mechanisms where some already exist”. The system is therefore scoped as a reference instrument — it surfaces and attributes what the governing documents say. It does not score partners, does not decide, and does not attempt to replace counsel or an ethics advisory board.

Scope and Objectives

In scope.

  • Natural-language questions answered strictly from an indexed corpus of ethical and legal instruments.
  • Answers attributed to the specific documents they draw on.
  • Jurisdiction awareness: global instruments always apply; EU and California regimes are opt-in, so a Californian company is not advised out of EU law it is not subject to.
  • Two question types: specific factual lookup, and broad synthesis across a whole document.
  • Fully local execution, including the language model.
  • User-supplied documents in addition to the shipped corpus.

Out of scope, deliberately, and consistent with the predecessor tool’s own stated boundaries (workshop slide 16) and the workshop’s caution against over-reach:

  • Scoring, ranking, or red-flagging partners.
  • Standalone decision-making, or any output framed as a decision.
  • Legal advice or a compliance attestation.
  • Replacing live discussion, an ethics advisory board, or counsel.

Objectives. The system is judged against three: (i) answers are grounded — supported by the corpus and attributed to it; (ii) questions the corpus cannot answer are refused rather than fabricated; and (iii) broad questions receive complete answers, not a plausible-sounding subset. Section 4 reports performance against each.

System Architecture

High-Level Design

The system is three containers: a Streamlit front end, a Chroma vector store, and an Ollama runtime hosting the language models. Nothing else is required, and no network egress occurs after the initial model download.

The query path is: classify the question, retrieve from the matching pool under the active jurisdiction filter, assemble a prompt within a token budget, and stream a grounded answer back with its sources.

Component Descriptions

Tokenization and context budgeting

The system does not implement a tokenizer. Tokenization happens inside the model runtime, and the application deliberately does not duplicate it. What the application does own is context budgeting, and it is worth being precise about the distinction because the naive approach fails silently.

Ollama defaults to a 2048-token context window regardless of the model’s actual capacity. Retrieved context that exceeds it is silently truncated — the request succeeds, the answer is simply built on less evidence than intended. The system therefore estimates prompt size (at roughly four characters per token, adequate for sizing) and sets num_ctx explicitly per request.

The same budget governs conversation history. Retrieved evidence and the current question are non-negotiable; whatever budget remains is filled with prior turns, newest first. History is dropped oldest-first only when the prompt would otherwise overflow, and the interface reports when it happens rather than truncating invisibly.

Language models

Four model roles are separated, each on a different model, all local:

Role Model Rationale
Chat / answering gpt-oss:20b Best measured grounding fidelity (Section 4.4)
Embedding nomic-embed-text Shared by ingestion and query, so both sit in one vector space
Mode classification llama3.2 Binary routing is easy; a small model keeps added latency small
Evaluation judge gemma4:12b Deliberately a different family from the chat model

The judge separation is a methodological safeguard: a model grading its own output shares its blind spots, so scores would be inflated in exactly the cases that matter. Generation runs at temperature=0 with a fixed seed for reproducibility, though bit-identical output is not achievable on this stack — see Section 4.3.

Retrieval

Retrieval is the component with the most design content, because it is where nearly every observed quality problem originated.

Two pools, not one. Chunks are tagged content (verbatim document excerpts) or summary (statements from a pre-built per-document digest). Specific questions search excerpts; broad questions search digests. The reason is measured, not stylistic: for the question “summarise the legal and ethical principles underlying responsible neurotechnology development”, similarity search over excerpts retrieved 0 of 3 known-relevant passages at k=50, and required k=200 of 521 chunks — 38% of the entire corpus — to reach 2 of 3. An abstract query matches abstract framing language, not the specific provisions that actually answer it. Digests solve this by making each retrievable unit a dense, self-contained statement.

Line-aware chunking. Chunks are packed on line boundaries rather than cut at fixed character offsets. Fixed-offset chunking had placed six unrelated definitions in one chunk, averaging its embedding across all six; the query “what is cognitive liberty?” then failed to retrieve the passage that literally defines it until k=50. After the fix, it retrieves at k=4.

Jurisdiction filtering. Every chunk carries a jurisdiction. Retrieval is restricted to Global plus whatever the user selects, so EU regulation cannot shape an answer for a company not subject to it.

Digests

Digests are the mechanism that makes broad questions answerable. Each document is read once, ahead of time, and reduced to a list of the statements it makes; those statements are stored as summary chunks.

Two properties are deliberate. First, digest generation is a separate, explicit command, never part of startup — it costs tens of minutes to hours of CPU inference per document, and a colleague’s first launch must not hang on it. Second, digests are files committed to the repository, not merely rows in the vector store. They therefore survive the vector store being rebuilt, ship with the application so nobody regenerates them, and can be produced by any model — including a frontier model via a web interface, which is how the current corpus digests were produced.

That second property was learned the hard way: an early metadata migration wiped the collection and destroyed hours of generated digests, because they existed only inside Chroma. File-backed digests make that failure unreachable.

Guardrails

“Guardrails” here means a set of constraints on what the system will assert, not a separate moderation library.

  • Grounding instruction. The system prompt restricts answers to the supplied context and requires source attribution. It also, deliberately, instructs the model not to hedge when the context does support a conclusion — an earlier, stricter version caused the model to refuse questions it could actually answer.
  • Refusal. Questions the corpus cannot support are declined. This is measured as a first-class test case, not assumed.
  • Determinism. temperature=0 and a fixed seed.
  • Jurisdictional scoping, as above.
  • Visible routing. The mode actually used is displayed with each answer, so an automatic misroute is visible rather than silently producing a worse answer.
  • Graceful degradation. A broad question asked before digests exist falls back to excerpts and says so, rather than answering from an empty context.

Local storage and privacy

All state is local: documents on disk, vectors in a Docker volume, models in a second volume, conversation history in browser session state only and never persisted to the vector store. After the one-time model download, the stack requires no network access.

This is a functional requirement rather than a stance. The questions a company asks an ethics tool — about a specific partner, a contemplated use case, a regulatory exposure — are among the more sensitive things it could transmit.

Containerization

Three services under Docker Compose: app (Streamlit), chroma, and ollama. Named volumes persist models and vectors; data/documents is bind-mounted so documents can be added from the host. Models are pulled automatically on first run, so docker compose up --build is the only command a new user issues.

Frontend

A single-page Streamlit chat interface. The sidebar lists indexed documents by title, grouped by jurisdiction, with a selector for where the user operates. Answers stream token-by-token, followed by the mode used, the retrieved sources, and a copy-to-clipboard control for pasting into the user’s own documents.

Evaluation

Yardstick

Evaluation is deliberately split by stage, because a drop in end-to-end answer quality is otherwise unattributable — retrieval and generation fail for different reasons and have different remedies.

Layer What it measures Cost Where
Retrieval eval Does the known-correct passage reach the model at all? Seconds, no LLM evals/retrieval.py
Answer eval Is the answer correct, per case-specific criteria? Minutes evals/run.py
Regression tests Does the pipeline still behave? ~5 min, small model tests/

The gold dataset lives in evals/dataset.py and is the single source of truth; graders live in evals/graders.py. Both runners consume the same definitions, so a regression test and an eval score cannot disagree about what “correct” means. Every expected answer was verified against the document text before being recorded.

Three grader types are used. refusal checks that an unanswerable question is declined. contains checks that required content appears, with synonym groups so that phrasing is not over-constrained. judge asks a second model, one rubric item at a time, whether the answer conveys a specific principle — a narrow binary question a smaller model can answer reliably, unlike an open-ended “is this good?”.

Results by Mode

Question-answering mode: 5 of 6. Specific factual questions are reliably answered from verbatim excerpts at k=4 — the corpus is searched at low cost and the correct passage is returned.

Broad-principles mode: 14 of 14. The open synthesis question — the one the system was least able to answer before digests existed — now scores full marks against an independent judge on a rubric of fourteen principles the corpus states. For scale, the same question answered by naive retrieval covered roughly a third of them.

Common Failure Modes

Retrieval loses to boilerplate. The one reproducible failure, oecd-due-regard, asks what societal values the OECD document says should be shown due regard. The answer is one clause of principle 1(d). The query names the document — “the OECD document” — and that phrase matches the OECD’s front and back matter far more strongly than its provisions. All six top-ranked chunks are the “About the OECD” section, citation lists, and the Compendium blurb. The provision never enters the context.

This failure reproduces identically across four different chat models, which is the strongest available evidence that it is a retrieval defect rather than a reasoning one. The remedy is to strip boilerplate sections at ingestion — now straightforward, since the markdown conversion gave every document real headings.

Grader brittleness can masquerade as system failure. An initial scorecard read 4 of 7. Two of those three failures were defects in the graders: the chat model wrote “self‑determination” with a non-breaking hyphen, defeating an ASCII substring match on an answer that was exactly correct; and it refused the cake question with a phrasing absent from the refusal-phrase list. Both were real bugs, but in the measuring instrument. This is recorded here because the failure mode is insidious: an eval that under-reports looks like diligence.

Determinism is behavioural, not bit-exact. At temperature=0 the model makes the same answer-versus-refuse decision every time, but token-identical output is not achievable: multithreaded floating-point reductions in the inference backend are non-associative, so near-tied logits can flip between runs. The regression tests assert the former and not the latter.

More context is not monotonically better. Retrieval recall plateaus between k=8 and k=30 on this corpus — every chunk past the eighth adds cost without adding evidence. Measured end-to-end, k=30 produced a less complete answer than k=12, consistent with the “lost in the middle” effect (Liu et al. 2023). This is why the modes retrieve 4 and 12 chunks rather than as many as fit.

Corpus growth dilutes broad retrieval. With seven documents’ digests competing, the broad case needs a larger k to surface the same passages it found when two documents were indexed. The graded answer still scored 14/14, so this has not yet cost quality — but it is a scaling pressure to watch.

Trade-offs

Model size versus latency. gemma3:4b runs the full suite in 40% of the time of gpt-oss:20b and still reaches 12 of 14 on broad synthesis. The default remains gpt-oss:20b because grounding fidelity is the system’s purpose, but on constrained hardware the smaller model is a defensible choice — and notably, it fails nowhere that gpt-oss:20b does not also fail.

CPU inference is the binding constraint. With no GPU, answers take tens of seconds to a few minutes. Thread count was tested directly and is not a usable lever: forcing all sixteen logical cores was 2.1× slower than the runtime’s own auto-detection, because the two SMT threads per physical core contend for the same execution units. There is no free configuration win here.

Digest generation cost versus coverage. Digests are what make broad questions work, and they are expensive: the EU AI Act alone was estimated at roughly thirteen hours of local CPU inference. The resolution was to decouple generation from the application — digests are portable files that can be produced anywhere, by any model, and committed. Local generation remains supported for users who cannot use an external model, at a cost in time rather than in capability.

Local-only versus quality. Running a 20B-parameter model on a laptop CPU costs real answer quality relative to a frontier model. This is accepted deliberately: the confidentiality of the questions is the system’s reason for existing, and an architecture that sent them elsewhere would not be the same product.

Multi-query retrieval: a rejected approach. Decomposing a broad question into targeted sub-queries was implemented and measured. It produced no recall improvement, ran roughly 400× slower than single-query retrieval, and generated sub-queries containing vocabulary absent from the corpus. It is retained in the codebase but disabled by default. It is recorded here because the negative result is informative: the fix for broad questions was better source material (digests), not cleverer querying.

Appendix: Corpus

The indexed corpus comprises 3,630 content chunks and 173 digest chunks across seven instruments. All seven are stored as markdown; conversion from PDF was necessary rather than cosmetic, as extraction from the Official Journal typesetting of the GDPR corrupted roughly one word in four (ar ticle, p aragraph, author ised), which would have made that document effectively unsearchable.
