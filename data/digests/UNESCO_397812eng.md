The General Conference of UNESCO adopted the Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology at its 43rd session in Samarkand on 11 November 2025.
The Recommendation addresses the current and potential positive and negative impacts of neurotechnology on human lives, including the human mind, and on human health and well-being, societies, the environment and ecosystems.
The Recommendation is grounded in UNESCO's constitutional mandate to promote collaboration among nations in order to further universal respect for justice, the rule of law, human rights and fundamental freedoms.
The Recommendation is a standard-setting instrument developed through a global approach, based on international law, and focused on human dignity, human rights, gender equality, social and global justice, sustainable development, physical and mental well-being, diversity, global solidarity, fairness, non-discrimination, inclusiveness and environmental protection.
The Recommendation recognizes the significant and growing global prevalence of neurological and mental health conditions and the suffering they cause.
The Recommendation acknowledges the potential of neurotechnology to offer innovative solutions for preventive, predictive, diagnostic, therapeutic and rehabilitative purposes.
The Recommendation considers that neurotechnology raises ethical, legal and societal issues concerning human dignity and human rights, such as autonomy, privacy, mental and physical integrity, personal identity, freedom of thought, risk of discrimination, inequality and challenges to democracy.
The Recommendation recognizes the ethically significant distinction between the medical and non-medical use of neurotechnology and between its use as treatment or with the aim of enhancement.
The Recommendation recognizes that it is ethically imperative to explore and harness the potential of neurotechnology, particularly for medical and assistive use.
The Recommendation reaffirms the right of everyone to share in scientific advancement and enjoy its benefits.
The Recommendation underlines that no country and no one should be left behind in access to neurotechnology and its benefits or in protection against its risks, while respecting the desire of some people not to take part in all technological developments.
The Recommendation emphasizes that specific attention should be paid to lower-middle-income countries, including least developed countries, landlocked developing countries and small island developing States, which have been underrepresented in the development of and access to neurotechnology.
The Recommendation underlines that global cooperation and solidarity facilitate fair and equitable access to neurotechnology while mitigating risks of dual use, misuse or malicious use.
The Recommendation recalls that the General Conference mandated the Director-General by 42 C/Resolution 29 to prepare a standard-setting instrument on the ethics of neurotechnology in the form of a recommendation.
The Recommendation bears in mind the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the instruments of the international human rights framework and other relevant international instruments, recommendations and declarations.
The Recommendation recalls that the obligation and primary responsibility to promote and protect human rights lie with the State, and that business enterprises, including technology companies, have a responsibility to respect human rights, including through human rights due diligence, in line with the United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights.
The Recommendation recognizes that neurotechnology may lack adequate regulation and that effective measures are needed to address its adverse human rights impacts.
The General Conference recommends that Member States apply the provisions of the Recommendation by taking appropriate steps, including legislative or other measures, in conformity with their constitutional practice and governing structures and with international law.
The General Conference recommends that Member States engage the multi-stakeholder community in the implementation of the Recommendation and bring it to the attention of international, regional and national authorities and bodies, research and academic organizations, and public, private and civil society institutions involved in neurotechnology.
The nervous system includes the central and the peripheral nervous system, and nervous system activity is the basis of sensory, motor and mental states and supports consciousness, sleep and the experience of pain.
Neurotechnology refers to devices, systems and procedures, encompassing both hardware and software, that directly measure, access, monitor, analyse, predict or modulate the nervous system to understand, influence, restore or anticipate its structure, activity and function.
Neurotechnology spans medical and non-medical applications and includes tools that measure, infer and influence nervous system activity and mental states, whether through direct interaction with the nervous system, invasive or non-invasive, or by interfacing it with devices and systems.
Both open-loop and closed-loop systems introduce complex ethical issues because they affect physical and mental processes and may have delayed effects.
Medical use refers to any application of neurotechnology intended for preventive, predictive, diagnostic, therapeutic or rehabilitative purposes in relation to a health condition, in accordance with internationally accepted medical standards.
Neurotechnology includes technical tools that measure and analyse physical, chemical and biological signals of the nervous system, as well as technical or interventional tools that interact with the structure or functions of the nervous system to change its activity.
Neural data include qualitative and quantitative data about the structure, activity and function of the nervous system gathered through neurotechnology, encompassing both direct measurements and indirect functional indicators, many of which are correlated with mental states.
Technologies that collect indirect neural data and non-neural data allowing mental state inferences raise ethical and human rights issues similar to those raised by neurotechnology, and the Recommendation applies to them accordingly.
Neurotechnology should be considered across its whole life cycle, from conception, design, materials, research and development to deployment, use, maintenance, monitoring, end-of-use, disposal and recycling, including its convergence with other technologies and the diversity of actors involved.
The Recommendation addresses ethical issues related to the whole life cycle of neurotechnology and its impacts on human health, well-being, individuals, communities, societies, the environment, ecosystems and the enjoyment of human rights.
The Recommendation approaches the ethics of neurotechnology as a systematic normative reflection based on a holistic, multicultural, multidisciplinary, pluralistic and evolving framework of interdependent human rights-based values, principles and actions.
The Recommendation recognizes that interventions involving the nervous system are particularly sensitive because the nervous system is the coordinating centre of behaviour and mental processes and enables the exercise of individual autonomy.
The Recommendation addresses the convergence of neurotechnology with other technologies such as spatial computing, immersive technologies, extended reality, AI, sensors and semiconductors.
The Recommendation addresses the integration of AI with neurotechnology and the associated risks, including cybersecurity concerns, lack of transparency, algorithmic bias, and risks to autonomy, agency, mental privacy, personal identity, and of surveillance, addiction and manipulation.
The Recommendation promotes the peaceful use of neurotechnology, raises awareness of risks of dual use, misuse or malicious use, and urges all concerned to respect international law, including international humanitarian law and international human rights law.
The Recommendation aims to guide the whole life cycle of neurotechnology in ways that are ethical, responsible, safe, secure, transparent, trustworthy and effective for peaceful use and the good of humanity, and to prevent harm in the present and the future in compliance with international law.
The objectives of the Recommendation include ensuring the protection, respect and promotion of human rights, fundamental freedoms, human dignity and equality, safeguarding the interests of present and future generations, preserving the environment and promoting sustainable development.
The Recommendation aims to guide the actions of Member States, individuals, groups, communities, institutions, research organizations, private sector companies and every other relevant actor to ensure that ethical principles and international human rights law are upheld at all stages of the neurotechnology life cycle.
The Recommendation aims to promote the responsible development and use of neurotechnology to provide innovative healthcare and the best possible diagnostic and therapy options.
The Recommendation requires that neurotechnology in its whole life cycle be science- and evidence-based, reliable and reproducible, and continuously evaluated for safety, effectiveness, efficiency, accessibility and quality.
The Recommendation aims to provide a universal framework of values and principles that translates into concrete policy recommendations and effective implementation for Member States.
The Recommendation aims to foster inclusive multi-stakeholder, multidisciplinary and pluralistic dialogue about ethical issues relating to neurotechnology.
The Recommendation aims to promote justice and equitable access to developments and knowledge in neurotechnology and the equitable sharing of benefits, with particular attention to lower-middle-income countries, least developed countries, landlocked developing countries and small island developing States.
The Recommendation recognizes that tensions may arise between its values and principles and that a contextual assessment, respecting proportionality and human rights, is necessary to manage such tensions.
The inherent dignity of every human being is the foundation of universal human rights, and neurotechnology should never be used in ways that undermine the dignity or rights of any individual, including those living in vulnerable situations.
The research, development and application of neurotechnology that promotes comprehensive human health and well-being should be prioritized, viewing health as a holistic state of physical, mental and social well-being.
The responsible allocation of resources for neurotechnology should prioritize preventive, diagnostic, therapeutic, assistive and rehabilitative purposes that benefit the largest number of people, and should prioritize people in underserved areas and who are least well off.
Respect for diversity and fairness should be upheld throughout the whole life cycle of neurotechnology.
Equitable access to evidence-based and reliable neurotechnology should be prioritized globally, regardless of socioeconomic status, geographical location or any other grounds, with special attention to lower-middle-income countries, resource-constrained settings, persons in vulnerable situations, persons with disabilities and persons with neurological disorders or mental health conditions.
The use of neurotechnology should be particularly scrutinized to avoid uses that segregate, discriminate, stigmatize, objectify or subordinate individuals or communities or that exacerbate existing inequalities or generate novel inequalities.
Research and development involving diverse groups and communities should be undertaken with their permission, guidance and prior, free and informed consent, in partnership and in ways that respect their traditional knowledge and epistemic contributions.
The Recommendation guides all actors in the neurotechnology life cycle to act in solidarity and calls for accountability where neurotechnology is used in ways that threaten or violate international law, and international cooperation is essential to address cross-border issues.
Neurotechnology should be developed and used with respect for environmental stewardship, minimizing ecological harm throughout the life cycle of the materials used, including extraction, data processing and storage, recycling and disposal.
Neurotechnology throughout its life cycle should be guided by respect for the rights of Indigenous Peoples as set forth in the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.
All actors in the neurotechnology life cycle should adhere to ethical standards, take responsibility for their actions, be accountable for outcomes and take corrective action when necessary.
Scientific integrity throughout the neurotechnology life cycle requires science- and evidence-based, objective and transparent research practices and transparent evaluation of research outputs.
Neurotechnology should promote health and well-being and empower individuals to make free and informed decisions concerning their nervous system and mental health.
Neurotechnology should contribute to human health and well-being without causing avoidable harm, whether physical, economic, social, political, cultural or mental, and the "do no harm" principle must guide its whole life cycle.
Any restrictions on the exercise and enjoyment of human rights must meet all applicable requirements under international law.
The principles of proportionality, balance and legitimacy should govern the use of neurotechnology and the data it enables, ensuring that use is appropriate and proportional to the objective and expected benefits, consistent with the values of the Recommendation, appropriate to the context and target user group, and based on safety principles and rigorous scientific evidence.
Throughout the whole life cycle of neurotechnology, the protection and promotion of autonomy and freedom of thought must be secured and mental and physical integrity protected from unwanted and harmful interference.
Autonomy is understood as both individual and relational, reflecting a person's self-determination and the community ties that give meaning to their choices.
Individuals are entitled and should be empowered to make free, informed and voluntary decisions about their engagement with neurotechnology, and implicit and explicit coercion to use neurotechnology should be safeguarded against, with special attention to children, adolescents and persons in vulnerable situations.
Consent to the use of neurotechnology should be prior, free and informed; informed consent procedures should be affirmative, dynamic, iterative, comprehensive, transparent and adequately documented, and should provide understandable, detailed and accessible information about purposes, risks, benefits, alternatives and outcomes.
Informed consent and assent should be adapted to the individual's age, decision-making capacity, culture, language, education level and mental and physical condition; for persons unable to give consent, the authorization of legal representatives or guardians should be sought, respecting the will and preferences of the person represented.
Informed consent must always include the entitlement to refuse or withdraw from using neurotechnology at any time, particularly where individuals are in a power imbalance situation.
Neurotechnology should never be used to exert undue influence or manipulation, whether through force, coercion, perception of disadvantage, social pressure or other means that compromise autonomy and freedom of thought, covering both the internal processing of thoughts and their external expression.
Neurotechnology and other technologies that collect indirect neural data and non-neural data allowing mental state inferences raise issues pertaining to the right to privacy because of their ability to collect uniquely sensitive data about the nervous system.
Privacy, including mental privacy, is fundamental for personal identity and agency and for the protection of human dignity.
The collection, processing, modification and sharing of neural data, indirect neural data and non-neural data allowing mental state inferences require the prior, free and informed consent of the person concerned, with the exception of life-threatening medical emergency situations as recognized in international frameworks.
There should be clear safeguards against the collection and misuse or unauthorized access or processing of neural data, including prior, free and informed consent, data minimization and purpose limitation, autonomy over data, including the ability to access, correct, erase and suspend processing, and data security and protection, particularly where such data might be aggregated with other sources.
All actors in the neurotechnology life cycle, particularly at its interface with AI, have a shared responsibility to ensure that these technologies do not perpetuate or amplify existing or new forms of inequality or discrimination based on neurological or mental characteristics or any other grounds.
The development of standards for neurotechnology should be inclusive in order to preserve human dignity, protect cultural and collective identities, prevent homogenization and respect neurodiversity, and no one may be discriminated against because they do not use or refuse to use neurotechnology.
Governments and neurotechnology stakeholders should engage in transparent and concrete public dialogue with citizens, public organizations and other stakeholders and include them in decision-making; social acceptance of neurotechnology uses should not replace individuals' prior, free and informed consent.
All actors in the neurotechnology life cycle should be transparent about their activities and should anticipate and address potential harms, and should be accountable for the outcomes of neurotechnology.
Broad and equitable access to neurotechnology should be promoted, and neurotechnology should be used to reduce global health inequalities and improve health particularly in resource-limited settings.
Neurotechnology should be deployed in a way that promotes the holistic development of children, consistent with international human rights law, and its use on children should be limited to medical, therapeutic or other well-justified purposes.
Member States should ensure suitable oversight of neurotechnology use cases, including that neurotechnology is not used for social control or surveillance.
Member States should establish structures and mechanisms for evaluating the potential impacts of neurotechnologies across their entire life cycle.
Member States should ensure that data protection, privacy and cybersecurity frameworks are sufficiently broad and robust to address issues arising from neurotechnology, particularly the collection and use of highly sensitive neural data.
Member States should ensure that the energy use of neurotechnology, including large-scale data centres and computing resources, has a sustainable ecological footprint, including through data minimization and energy efficiency.
Member States should establish community education and engagement programmes, including to inform the public about the potential negative impacts of neurotechnology.
Member States should ensure that neurotechnology researchers adhere to rigorous ethical standards for research and development.
Member States should protect vulnerable categories of individuals, including children, older persons, persons with physical disabilities and persons with mental health conditions, from harms most relevant to them, including by prohibiting certain marketing techniques using neural data in relation to children and incentivizing the development of neurotechnology that enhances the quality of life of persons with disabilities.
Member States should differentiate between therapeutic and enhancement uses of neurotechnology.
In the health sector, Member States should promote reliable, safe and durable neurotechnology, establish robust oversight mechanisms for the physical and mental health impacts of neurotechnology, and strengthen existing reporting systems to track adverse effects.
In the education and employment context, neurotechnology should not focus solely on improving academic achievement and should respect workers' rights.
In the commercial and consumer context, existing consumer protection principles should apply equally to neurotechnology.
Member States should establish rules that expressly prohibit the use of neural data in recommender systems for manipulative purposes, restrict the use of neural data for nudging, prohibit the use of neurotechnology for marketing during sleep, and impose specific rules on neuromarketing.
Member States should prohibit any direct or indirect pressure on individuals to use neurotechnological enhancement, prohibit enhancement uses that undermine human dignity, identity or equal opportunity, and develop guidance on which enhancement applications are acceptable, which are off limits, and which require strict conditions and oversight.
UNESCO will support Member States in implementing the Recommendation through tools such as a Readiness Assessment Methodology, Ethical Impact Assessment frameworks and capacity-building programmes.
